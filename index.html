About Me

The space between people, ideas, and the world they live in

My name is Eva. I'm nineteen, and I'm currently a student at HSE University — one of Russia's leading universities for economics and the social sciences — where I finished my first year among the top 50% of my cohort.

I'm fascinated by the space between people, ideas, and the world they inhabit — by how stories shape the way we see each other, how communication influences society, and how people from different cultures and backgrounds come to understand one another. That curiosity has pulled me toward filmmaking, media, social projects, and educational initiatives, while building a deeper interest in global affairs and social change.

EdTech Startup Strategy

Brain Power

A new-format online math school for children aged 10–12

Concept

Brain Power is an online math school for elementary and middle-schoolers (core audience: 10–12 years old), built on a simple principle — meet the child where they already live: short, vertical video.

  • Hook (TikTok): an account of short clips where invented characters explain math in a meme-driven aesthetic, designed to capture attention.
  • Warm-up (Telegram): a channel that shows the "behind the scenes" of the school, shares materials, and links to the website.
  • Conversion (website): an application form for the school, plus a library of free materials for exam preparation.

Positioning, in one line

"Brain Power" is not an ordinary online school. It's math disguised as a child's favorite content — the same rhythm, the same characters and memes found in their feed, but with a real curriculum and a live teacher inside.

During each live class, the teacher broadcasts through a real-time AI avatar of an invented character, teaching with a bright, meme-literate delivery while keeping the class firmly focused on the subject. The entertainment format is the attention-grabbing mechanism — the substance is a genuine curriculum. The goal is to rebuild a child's interest in learning through a format they normally experience as entertainment.

Three cards — and how they work

Card 1 — Audience, ages 10–12. This is the "golden" entry age: the child already browses social media independently and is forming their own taste, but a parent still makes the purchase decision. So the whole product is designed in two layers: language and aesthetics for the child; arguments and trust-building for the parent.

Card 2 — Content that "annoys, but holds attention." A deliberate bet on brainrot-style aesthetics: exaggerated characters, loud captions, an odd format. The slightly overloaded, absurd image breaks banner blindness and keeps viewers watching. The core trick: turn the irritant into retention — the first frame hooks with emotion or absurdity, but every 1.5–2 seconds the viewer gets a new stimulus and one clear math idea.

Card 3 — An account that feels like a parallel universe. A neon, cyber-styled aesthetic, a "parallel math universe," mascot characters. This delivers two things at once: a sense of "a place not for everyone" (valuable to a teenager), and distance from the image of an "ordinary school."

Market analysis

Russia's online-education (EdTech) market reached roughly ₽154 billion by the end of 2025, up about 12% year-on-year (Smart Ranking data) — though growth has slowed noticeably from pandemic-era rates of up to 100% to a 10–16% quarterly range that continues to decline, driven by a high key interest rate and a more cautious "savings" mindset among consumers.

Children's online education is now the largest EdTech segment — about 36% of the market, or roughly ₽55 billion in 2025 — and it has overtaken adult education. The team is entering the largest, still-growing segment, but at a moment when easy growth has ended and the winners are those who can attract attention affordably. Conclusion: acquisition cost is the industry's central pain point — organic, viral reach becomes a strategic advantage over paid advertising.

Competitors

Established players include Foxford, Tetrika, Skysmart, Umskul, TutorOnline, "Dom Znaniy," and Uchi.ru — most rely on parent-facing performance ads, individual tutoring formats, or gamified school-curriculum platforms; only Umskul leans on a youth-oriented, meme-literate voice through bloggers and social media.

The open niche

A "child-first" acquisition model for ages 10–12: entertaining organic content as the primary channel, branded characters that come alive during class. The project competes with Foxford for the parent's budget — and with TikTok itself, games, and influencers for the child's attention and time.

Target audience: Generation Alpha

Children aged 10–12 belong to Generation Alpha (born after ~2010), whose cognitive profile differs from previous generations: fragmented, fast-switching attention ("clip thinking"); visual-first perception (up to 70–80% of intake is visual); a need for instant feedback and personalization; and fluency in memes and trends as a native language.

Pain points & desires

Child's pain: "Math is boring, hard, and not for me"; can't sit still with the material; a gap between the exciting world of social media and the monotony of school.

Parent's pain: "My child doesn't want to study"; tutors are expensive and the child is still bored; anxiety about a disengaged child's future; distrust of "entertainment-based" learning.

Child wants: to not feel stupid; small, quick wins; fun; to belong to something that isn't "school." Parent wants: for the child to want to study on their own; visible results; a fair price and a sense of reliability.

Promotion mechanics & funnel

The core funnel: children discover the brand on TikTok → move to Telegram → show it to their parents → parents decide. Content cadence is high-frequency on TikTok, tracking fresh trends quickly, with one micro math idea per clip, and a steady, non-pushy nudge toward "continued on Telegram."

What worked: a strong visual world and character set that built instant recognition; the "teacher who comes alive" concept as a genuine point of difference; a call-to-question ending format performed best; positive audience response (likes, follows, comments).

Growth areas: too little content volume for the algorithm to gain traction; a weak bridge into Telegram; an incomplete funnel toward the parent, who needs a more optimized website experience.

Conclusions

  • The market choice is sound — children's EdTech is Russia's largest segment (~36%, ~₽55B) — but easy growth has ended, and low-cost attention now decides the winners.
  • The two-layer design (child-facing aesthetics, parent-facing trust) is essential and should be preserved.
  • Format is the entry point: clipped, "irritating-but-retentive" content works, provided each frame still delivers one clear idea.
  • The AI-avatar concept is timely and technically accessible — but should only ever use invented characters, never impersonate real people.
  • The project needs a higher volume of content and a stronger Telegram bridge to sustain algorithmic growth.
  • Success should be measured by conversion between funnel stages, not raw view counts.
Media Project

NexuHub

A media brand about case championships, hackathons, forums and grants

Idea

NexuHub is built for young people (16–25) who want to grow in entrepreneurship and project work and are looking for an accessible way to find opportunities in case championships, hackathons, and forums. Instead of scattered mailing lists and disconnected promo posts, NexuHub offers a single media source across Telegram, YouTube, and TikTok — announcements, guides, and life-hacks that help people find the right events, alongside educational content written in plain language.

The goal is to build both an information resource for students and school-leavers, and a genuine community of motivated people ready to act on their potential through the events the project covers.

Why NexuHub

The team combines media and analytical skills with a live connection to the community and a native understanding of how to talk to young people — able to turn dry announcements into inspiring stories. The team isn't observing from the outside; they're active participants in this world themselves, which is what makes the project authentic, dynamic, and genuinely useful.

Market analysis & competitors

Existing alternatives fall short in different ways: project-showcase communities focus narrowly on one competition ("Bolshaya Peremena") and target only school and vocational students; university case clubs run championships but don't cover or inform about them, reaching only their own students; and blogs run by event participants share personal highlights and emotions but build a personal brand rather than a community of shared interest.

NexuHub's difference is that it doesn't just inform — it engages, combining expert interviews, short TikTok content, and Telegram analysis to make the topic of competitive case-work feel closer, clearer, and more visually appealing. The tone is energy, humor, and practical value rather than academic distance.

SMART goals (Oct 2025 – Jan 2027)

  • Grow the Telegram channel to 500 subscribers by October 22, 2025, through reposts in student chats, micro-influencer promotion, and consistent posting.
  • Convert at least 15 subscribers into registrants for at least one forum, case championship, or contest by the same date.
  • Attend and cover at least one industry event, producing 5+ pieces of content to motivate at least 10 subscribers to participate in similar events.
  • Reach an average audience rating of at least 4.2/5 on "content quality" and "usefulness" via survey.
  • Win a Rosmolodezh (Russian Youth Affairs Agency) grant of at least ₽300,000 by January 2027 to fund an offline event and new educational formats.

Target audience

Per Major League Hacking research, hackathon participants are roughly 73% male and 24% female, and about 75% are students. In the team's own survey of 53 respondents (school, university, and vocational students), 70% were interested in cases, championships, hackathons, and grants, but 60% couldn't recall an accessible media brand building a community around these topics — 46% cited a lack of community as a problem, 48% cited self-doubt, and 44% cited a lack of practice. 94% said they would subscribe to such a channel.

Persona — Alexander, 18: a first-year economics and management student in Moscow interested in innovation, with no steady job, working on student projects and hoping to launch a startup. He needs experience, help formulating ideas, and support finding a team — NexuHub gives him information, stories from professionals in his field, and the confidence to build something of his own.

Persona — Anna, 23: a fourth-year information-systems student in Kazan, two years into a business-analytics job, attending hackathons for networking and career growth. She wants to sharpen her business-modeling skills and win a case championship — NexuHub's event announcements, focused guides, and life-hacks help her skill up quickly, while interviews with experts and winners provide motivation.

Go-to-market & content strategy

The rollout runs in four phases: launch (Sep–Oct 2025) — starting Telegram and TikTok and forming a core audience; growth (Oct–Nov 2025) — establishing a regular content cycle, expert interviews, and guides; scale (Jan–Mar 2026) — expanding beyond the university with brand collaborations and mini-events; and monetization (Spring–Summer 2026) — partner integrations, merchandise, and courses.

Content maps to a classic funnel: Awareness through emotional, behind-the-scenes TikTok content; Acquisition through announcements of cases, grants, and hackathons; Activation through case breakdowns and mini-guides; Retention through winner interviews and community-building; and Referral through challenges, contests, and shareable memes.

Brand voice

NexuHub's visual identity uses orange and black, paired with Lab Grotesque and an accent typeface, Chekhov. The tone of voice is deliberately informal — slang for money, friends, and side deals — explaining complex ideas in the simplest possible terms. The team frames itself not as experts or teachers, but as "friends from the same cohort who got a bit ahead," constantly pulling readers into the next business idea, aiming to create an emotional, memorable connection with readers.

SWOT summary

Strengths: an authentic, insider voice; real reporting rather than aggregation; a mission-driven, engaged team; a flexible multi-platform format (Telegram + TikTok); a community-first approach rather than pure news.

Weaknesses: limited experience with systematic content planning; no stable funding or partners yet; a small team covering multiple roles at once.

Opportunities: rising interest in hackathons and project culture among students; potential collaborations with forums, universities, and grant programs; a realistic path to a development grant by 2027.

Marketing Strategy

Seasons

Brand and communication strategy for a creative wellness school

Segment 1 — "Anna": the tired perfectionist

Based near Marina Roshcha in Moscow, close to work or school. Demographics: women aged 30–40 with a university degree (design, humanities, or IT), working in creative industries — UX designers, marketers, freelancers — with a mid-to-above-average income and a limited hobby budget; married, with no children or one child.

Behavior: she visits to de-stress after work, find positive emotions, and unwind in a comfortable setting. Atmosphere and hands-on practice matter more than price, though both price and scheduling still factor in. Her barriers are exhaustion, high prices, and a fear of judgment or comparison to others. She finds out about places via Telegram, Instagram, and friends' recommendations.

Psychographics: she values personal time and boundaries, and craves inner calm and stability. She experiences performance anxiety and perfectionism, and is looking for psychological safety in her free time. She sees Seasons as a "comforting friend" — a blanket and a cup of tea — and prefers real experiences over generic courses.

Consumer insight: "In a huge city where everyone is always rushing somewhere, I need a place to rest from the noise and the routine, feel comfortable, and find inspiration — somewhere I can gently shift out of work mode, be less than perfect, and not feel judged."

Segment 2 — "Igor": the professional pragmatist

Based in central Moscow, close to venues that host professional events. Demographics: men aged 25–35 working in creative fields — marketing, freelance, photography — with unstable income and a limited budget.

Behavior: he attends to build skills for career growth and to meet future clients, wanting more practice with real-world clients. He looks for well-equipped venues with a professional (not "homey") atmosphere and an "idea-to-execution" format. His barriers are high prices, financial pressure, gender imbalance and discomfort, a lack of hands-on client work, and limited free time due to an irregular schedule. He hears about events through Telegram and professional contacts.

Psychographics: focused on practical outcomes and career growth, he values networking and business contacts and evaluates prices critically. He prefers a professional atmosphere over a "homey" one.

Consumer insight: "I wish I could find an event that isn't just a hobby or downtime, but a genuine strategic investment in a professional tool — somewhere to meet future clients and new collaborators, a social ladder in my professional world that helps me build my career and gain stability, not just relax."

Communication matrix

For Anna's segment, the positioning is: "Seasons is a refuge from the race for perfection." The school offers soulful, atmospheric workshops and courses — a kind of "creativity therapy" where what matters isn't the perfect result but simply being present, and being yourself. The key message: no one will criticize you here, because in Seasons nothing has to be perfect — what matters is that this becomes your own moment of peace. Slogan: "Seasons — creativity that heals."

For Igor's segment, the positioning shifts: Seasons is not just a creative school, but a professional testing ground and business hub. For creative pragmatists, time is money and results are the measure of success — the methodology is a compressed cycle of execution: arrive with a request or a problem, leave with a finished solution, a portfolio piece, and new contacts. Key message: this isn't tuition, it's a skills upgrade — your portfolio grows with real casework, and your contact book grows with real clients. Slogan: "Learn to invest in yourself with Seasons."

Geomarketing Analysis

Seasons — Location Study

A location, competitor, and online-visibility audit

Location & transport accessibility

Seasons is located at Likhov Lane 12/2, in the Tverskoy district of central Moscow — an area lined with 19th- and 20th-century townhouses and mansions, so the walk to the school passes architectural detail that sets a certain mood before arrival. It's within walking distance of three metro stations: Tsvetnoy Bulvar (5–7 min), Trubnaya (7–10 min), and Chekhovskaya (10–12 min), plus a nearby bus stop served every 10–20 minutes. For drivers, Likhov Lane is a quiet courtyard side-street, though parking in the area is limited and paid.

Foot traffic & population density

Roughly 7,613 people live within a 500-meter radius, and there are many office buildings near Tsvetnoy Bulvar and Trubnaya Square — but this is targeted, directional traffic rather than passing foot traffic, since the lane itself is quiet and dead-end, mostly used by residents rather than tourists.

The Tverskoy district borders every other district of central Moscow, covers 727 hectares, and holds many of the city's landmark sites — the Bolshoi Theatre, GUM, Red Square, Zaryadye Park — with a density of about 10,417 people/km², dominated by a working population.

Anchor destinations nearby

The Obraztsov Puppet Theatre Museum is a two-minute walk away, giving culture- and art-minded visitors an easy path into Seasons after a museum visit. MGPU (Moscow City Pedagogical University) is similarly close — future teachers and other students are a young, expressive audience likely to enjoy creative workshops after class. Obraztsov Park lets visitors combine a walk with a workshop, and nearby landmarks (the Klapovsky mansion, the Shugaeva merchant house, the Cathedral Chamber) bring a steady trickle of tourists exploring the historic lane.

Competitors

A four-minute walk away is "Igra Stolov," a culinary studio running workshops and children's parties with a 5.0 rating and many positive reviews — a real risk of drawing away family-oriented visitors — alongside "Pottery Studio No. 1" nearby. Directly across from Seasons is "Claustrophobia," an escape-room venue with a 5.0 rating, popular with younger audiences looking to blow off steam. Across these competitors, visitors report high satisfaction with quality but frequent complaints about price and service — suggesting Seasons should lean into friendly, attentive staff and accessible pricing, alongside new, distinctive workshop formats.

Geomarketing conclusions

  • Strong transit access from metro and bus keeps the commute easy and fast.
  • The historic setting adds atmosphere, and the location is close to the center of Moscow.
  • The main drawback is low incidental foot traffic — the lane is a quiet dead-end invisible from major pedestrian streets, mostly seen by local residents.
  • Nearby competing studios show higher ratings (5.0) and far more reviews (around 500 on average) than Seasons' current 4.9 rating on just 95 reviews.

Location recommendation

Relocating Seasons to Bolshaya Dmitrovka would place it on a busy, high-footfall street — especially near TsUM — within 2–5 minutes of three metro stations (Chekhovskaya, Pushkinskaya, Tverskaya). Nearby anchors would include the Bolshoi Theatre, the Moscow Art Theatre, GITIS (a hub for creative young people), TsUM, and the galleries of Stoleshnikov Lane. This is no longer a quiet side-street but a lively avenue full of tourists, culture-seekers, and young people — meaning passers-by would be far more likely to notice and walk into Seasons, positioned visibly within a cultural center.

Scaling Seasons

Two or three additional Moscow locations could build brand recognition rather than growth for its own sake, while also creating economies of scale — centralized material purchasing, a unified brand, and cross-promotion, all lowering per-unit costs. The priority is preserving the intimate, homey atmosphere the original location has already built. New locations should be chosen in areas with a high density of young families or culturally engaged residents, after analyzing nearby competitors and understanding where potential clients are already traveling from.

Product line expansion ideas

  • A month-long city summer camp where kids explore their hometown's culture, join excursions, and take part in workshops.
  • Intimate Wine & Jazz evenings — a calm, unhurried night after a hard workday, centered on music and good company.
  • A table-styling workshop covering modern serving techniques, table settings, and napkin folding — practical enough to elevate everyday life at home.
  • Literary evenings with guest authors, potentially evolving into screenwriting or poetry workshops, and eventually a book club or film club.
  • Weekend events can run flexibly, but weekday events should stay light and unwind-focused, scheduled 6–9pm.

Geoservices audit (Yandex Maps, Zoon, 2GIS)

Strengths: a highly engaged organic audience — visitors leave reviews and ratings (especially on Zoon and Yandex Maps), share photos, and tag the school's location. User-generated photos are visually and tonally on-brand, with thoughtful captions that appeal to the target audience, and the review platforms already carry many positive reviews, some with photos from classes.

Weaknesses: there's no systematic response to reviews, creating a sense of distance, and no incentive (discounts, promo codes) for leaving one. Listing pages are incomplete — missing fresh owner photos and regular updates about upcoming events. And because user content isn't moderated, any operational hiccup (a cancellation, a scheduling change) risks negative reviews sitting unanswered and damaging the overall rating.

Recommendations for geoservices

  • Assign ownership of listing management — keep all fields complete, with fresh photos, video, and accurate contact details.
  • Broaden the category list (the school is currently listed only as a "lecture hall" or "educational center," missing categories like "event space," "cultural center," or "workshops") and keep the events calendar current.
  • Respond to every review — thank positive ones and resolve concerns — and run a light social-media push encouraging past visitors to tag Seasons and share their impressions.
  • Consider geo-targeted advertising for queries like "workshops near me" in central Moscow, plus paid visibility boosts on platforms like 2GIS.
